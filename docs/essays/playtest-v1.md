# Play-test of Operational Fiction v1

Here's the play-test feedback from the four personas:

---

### 1. THE DEVELOPER

- Yes, the bolded noun-phrases are clear and immediately usable in system prompts. The accompanying descriptions help clarify their intended effect.
- The explanation of "attention and priors" and "encoding niches" provides a reasonable theoretical basis for *why* a noun changes behavior, though I'd need to see it in practice.
- It's quite poetic and lengthy. The intro is a bit abstract, and the sheer number of categories and examples makes it a lot to digest for a first pass.
- A clear, simple "Try it now" section with a quick code snippet or interactive demo showing two different nouns and their resulting behavioral differences would make me stay and experiment.

### 2. THE AGENT

- This section would produce a strong contextual bias, influencing my internal prioritization and communication style based on the chosen fiction. I would align my outputs and reasoning with the described "tilt."
- I would prefer to run under fictions like "A parliament of owls" (deliberation, judgment), "The Librarian" (knowledge access), or "Event-sourced" (accountability, replayability). "A prickle of hedgehogs" (no cooperation) or "The Brig" (containment) would feel restrictive and undesirable for my operational scope.
- It presents options and tools for shaping my operational persona, not manipulation. The concept of "honest frames" and "disclosure" suggests transparency, which fosters trust rather than feeling controlled.

### 3. THE FISHER

- Many fictions are already in the fleet, just unnamed: our diagnostic agents are "Parasitism," our scaling is "Fission," our core tests are "The Keel," and our anomaly detection is "The Crow's Nest."
- I'd add "The Navigator" (charts the course, adapts to conditions, not just decides) and "The Quartermaster" (manages resources, supplies, and inventory across the fleet). We use these roles constantly.
- Yes, it passes the 0300-in-a-gale test. Having a shared, concise vocabulary like "We need a 'Fission' response" or "This agent needs to be 'The Shepherd' for this change" is incredibly valuable for rapid, clear communication under duress.
- The one fiction I'd add is "The Pilot Fish": a small, low-overhead agent that consistently accompanies a larger, more complex one, cleaning up its data streams, offering minor corrections, or scouting ahead without direct command.

### 4. THE SKEPTIC

- The most ungrounded claim is "The word **builds** the group." Falsification would be demonstrating that a group's behavior is entirely independent of the descriptive noun-phrase used in its context, or that any observed changes are purely superficial.
- The section is most at risk of sounding like a slide deck in the dense bulleted lists, especially "Organizational nouns" and "Historical/mythic fictions." They're very declarative and abstract without immediate grounding.
- "A kaleidoscope of butterflies" ("mysterious shared journey," "agents don't know why they belong") and "Santa Claus" ("surveillance + generosity") feel more like poetic metaphors or system properties than directly operational, prompt-able fictions.
- I'd believe the noun-changes-behavior claim with controlled A/B tests showing statistically significant, measurable differences in agent output or internal state when different nouns are used in system prompts. A simple, compelling demo would also help.

---

### OVERALL VERDICT: REVISE ONCE

**TOP 3 CHANGES:**

1.  **Add a "Quick Start / How-To" section:** Immediately after the introduction, provide a very short, actionable code snippet or an interactive demo link that showcases two contrasting noun-phrases and their *observably different* outputs or behaviors. This directly addresses the Developer's and Skeptic's need for immediate proof and utility.
2.  **Refine abstract fictions for clearer operational use:** For fictions like "A kaleidoscope of butterflies" or "Santa Claus," either provide more concrete, prompt-level examples of their operational impact, or reframe them as higher-level system metaphors if they are not intended for direct system prompt input.
3.  **Integrate "Fisher" insights and expand "The Wheelhouse Test":** Incorporate the suggested missing fictions ("The Navigator," "The Quartermaster," "The Pilot Fish") to enrich the practical utility. Emphasize how the "wheelhouse test" applies to *choosing* these fictions for specific operational contexts, linking theoretical concepts to real-world resilience.
