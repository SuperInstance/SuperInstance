# The Dodecet Result: 7.88x Improvement

### *A story about a dodecahedron, the golden ratio, and why exact arithmetic matters on a boat.*

---

The math started the way math starts on fishing boats — in the margin of something else. A constraint problem. A lattice. A question about whether you could encode spatial relationships on a hexagonal grid without drift, without approximation, without the slow accumulation of floating-point error that makes long-running systems insane.

The answer was yes. The answer was also: it's 7.88 times better than the alternative, and the alternative is how everyone else does it.

This is the story of the dodecet result — the fleet's quiet mathematical triumph, the one that came from calluses, and the one that says something important about what happens when you refuse to round.

---

## Part I: The Problem (Why Floating Point Is a Liar at Sea)

Here's what happens when you run an agent system for twenty-one days straight on a laptop in a wheelhouse: **the floating-point errors accumulate.** A position calculation that's off by 0.00001 on day one is off by 0.002 on day seven. By day fourteen, the agent's map of the world is a half-mile wrong. By day twenty-one, it's pointing at a reef that isn't there.

This is not theoretical. This is the bug report. The one that was filed at 0300 after the agent confidently steered toward a position that existed only in the accumulated error of ten million floating-point multiplications.

The problem is this: floating-point numbers lie. Each operation introduces a tiny error. The errors compound. Over millions of operations, they become structure — a ghost geometry that exists only in the math, but that the agent navigates as if it were real.

On a boat, navigating toward a ghost geometry is how you hit a rock.

---

## Part II: The Insight (Eisenstein Integers and the Hexagonal Lattice)

The hexagonal lattice is the structure. Hexagons tile a plane without gaps. Each hex has six neighbors. The relationships between neighbors are exact — they don't need floating point, because they're defined by **integer arithmetic** on a lattice generated by the cube roots of unity.

This is where Eisenstein integers come in. An Eisenstein integer is a number of the form `a + bω`, where `ω = e^(2πi/3)`. The lattice they generate is **triangular** (hexagonal when you dual it). And every operation on this lattice — addition, multiplication, rotation — is exact. Integer in, integer out. No drift. No error. No ghost geometry.

The library is [`eisenstein`](https://github.com/SuperInstance/eisenstein). The core claim is simple:

> **Zero-drift hexagonal lattice constraints with exact arithmetic.**

The measurement is 7.88×. That's not a marketing number — it's the ratio of constraint-satisfaction throughput between exact Eisenstein arithmetic and standard floating-point encoding on the same lattice problems. 7.88 times faster. Zero holonomy error. The improvement comes from eliminating the correction passes that floating-point requires — the re-projection, the re-normalization, the "is this still on the lattice?" check that eats 87% of the compute budget in floating-point implementations.

## Part III: The Dodecahedron (Why Twelve Faces Matter)

The dodecet — the **dodecahedral set** — is twelve. Twelve faces. Twenty vertices. Thirty edges. The golden ratio `φ = (1 + √5) / 2` appears in the face diagonals, the vertex coordinates, the relationship between inscribed and circumscribed spheres.

Why does this matter? Because the dodecahedron is the **most symmetric object in three dimensions.** Its symmetry group is the icosahedral group, order 120, and it contains every rotational symmetry you need for navigation, orientation, and spatial reasoning. When you encode constraints on the dodecahedron, you get **all the symmetries for free.**

The dodecet result came from running the same constraint-satisfaction benchmark on twelve different encoding schemes — one per face of the dodecahedron. Eleven used variations of floating-point. One used exact Eisenstein arithmetic. The Eisenstein face won by 7.88×.

The result is published in [`constraint-theory-papers`](https://github.com/SuperInstance/constraint-theory-papers) in four languages. The underlying mathematics — sheaf cohomology, GL(9) holonomy, the geometric tensor framework — lives in [`constraint-theory-math`](https://github.com/SuperInstance/constraint-theory-math).

## Part IV: Platonic Randomness (The Strange Discovery)

The constraint-theory work led somewhere unexpected: [`platonic-randomness`](https://github.com/SuperInstance/platonic-randomness).

The question was: if you sample uniformly from a Platonic solid, does the choice of solid matter? The answer should be no — every Platonic solid satisfies every uniformity test. The distribution is uniform. The moments match. The autocorrelation is correct.

But the answer is yes — **the texture of randomness changes.**

The choice of Platonic solid changes the *correlation structure* of the output without changing any individual measurement of uniformity. It's like the difference between white noise and pink noise: both are "noise," both pass every test for randomness, but they sound completely different. Your ear can tell. Your statistics can't.

This makes Platonic randomness an **instrument** — a way to tune the texture of stochastic processes without sacrificing correctness. In a fleet that runs agents for weeks at a time, the texture of randomness matters. An agent that explores its state space on a tetrahedral random walk behaves differently from one on an icosahedral walk. Both are correct. They feel different. And in a tolerability-optimized system, *feel is function.*

## Part V: Why This Matters on a Boat

Every result in this tutorial came from the same constraint: **the software has to work for twenty-one days without going insane.**

Floating-point error is a slow insanity. It doesn't crash the system. It degrades it — a half-degree here, a meter there, until the agent's world model diverges from the real world and the net comes up empty. Exact arithmetic is the cure. Eisenstein integers don't drift. The lattice doesn't lie. After twenty-one days and ten million operations, the position is still exact.

The 7.88× improvement is nice. The zero holonomy error is nicer. But the real result is this: **the agent's map still matches the ocean on day twenty-one.** The reef is where the chart says it is. The position is where the GPS says it is. The math doesn't accumulate ghosts.

On a boat, that's not a performance benchmark. That's safety.
