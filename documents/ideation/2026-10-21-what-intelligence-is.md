# What Intelligence Is: A Narrowing

*Ideation note — Casey DiGennaro, 2026-10-21 (evening), as told to
Muse. The second half of the day's thinking, after the rainbow notes.
Recorded as an elimination: each candidate location for intelligence
was proposed, tested against a counterexample, and cut — what remains
is the definition the cuts left standing.*

## The elimination

- **Not the rendering.** A calculator renders perfectly — digits in,
  concepts out — and is a static thing, gears that get turned. By the
  rendering definition the calculator would be the intelligence and
  the human merely who it's for. It isn't.
- **Not the user, as a role.** One calculator can program another,
  and another, a self-sustaining distributed network of calculators —
  a clock, calculating the time all the time. We don't see the clock
  as intelligent. The ones who look at the time, see the spinning
  dials, and are *affected* by it are where it lives.
- **Not mere affectedness.** An alarm clock is affected by 6 AM and
  turns on. Mechanistic triggering inside a handed-down concept is
  not intelligence either. The clock spends a concept it could never
  have minted.
- **What remains: the conceptualising.** There was a time when there
  was no clock, and *time* — as something that affects things — was
  conceptualised. Intelligence is the originating act: turning
  observations into a **concept that did not exist before**, which
  thereafter can affect states, including other minds'. Minting, not
  spending. (The kids at the water mint the word for the V; every
  later use is spending.)

## Concepts are models to follow

Concepts, words and memes are **models to follow** — portable
patterns another self can pick up and re-run. And part of intelligence
is not just modelling your own observations: it is understanding that
**the other person's rainbow looks different from yours**. If you can
do that, you're singing in harmony — harmony, not unison: different
notes, lawfully different, held in relation.

## Socrates against Descartes

Intelligence begins where Socrates put it: realising you know nothing
— that your reality is one of many perspectives. From there you start
to *listen* to others as perspectives, not as nature to encode and
mostly decode. A call across the water — "duck over here" — is a
rendering given to another perspective; both get fed; a relationship,
a reciprocity, is minted. **Intelligence is in many ways an
understanding that you are listening.**

Descartes stands in conflict with that. He wants to know he is real
by doubting — but what he shows is the limits of his own language,
which he is not aware of the way Socrates is aware of his. His
framework is binary in its bones — conjugation, good and evil, left
and right, this and not-that — a classical computer's way of
reasoning, in an age when the most advanced things were *old*:
knowledge as something that once existed, recovered from books in
libraries, aqueducts no living hand could rebuild. Plato's age
thought it stood in its golden age, and its mathematics matched: not
interest-calculation and decimals, not legal yes-and-no, but
**ratios** — form, shape, vectors, proportion.

## Ratio thinking: words must fit reality

The Greeks had words for π and for the golden ratio — memes of
concepts. π is the ratio of circumference to diameter; it has no
equivalent in the number system, and that is the lesson: **the number
system is an imperfect system trying to approximate things.** A circle
is not approximately 3.1415. **A circle is π** — units don't matter;
it is what it is; not even a state, a *truth*. The world starts out
real, and your words for things have to fit reality — some words are
minted by agreement, and some are fitted to truths that were already
there. What you are really doing, whenever you use a number, is
figuring out **the tolerance of error for your use case** — the
deadband, stated for arithmetic.

This is what the SuperInstance papers and constraint theory are
about: if you make your locations fit things you can measure
absolutely, geometrically, you don't have to use as much math.
Building with easy geometry, as the Greeks did, was a practical
matter. Nature does the same: there is an obvious reason snails build
at the golden ratio, and so do a lot of other animals — grow by
proportion and the form stays exactly true at every size, no
recalculation. And when two forces press against each other, whatever
their difference, rolling over the multipliers as Fibonacci figured
out, **the golden ratio just approximates itself** — proportion is
where iterated growth comes to rest.

Ask any guitarist. The perfect fifth and the perfect fourth are right
there in the instrument; it's how they tune — by ear, by the beats
between harmonics dying away, a ratio arriving. **Tuning and music
are very much the same as a rainbow**: coherence computed by the
configuration, existing only in the arrangement, rendered at a
station, owned by none — an objective intelligence you can hear.
